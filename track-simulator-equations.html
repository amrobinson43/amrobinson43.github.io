Equations, Variables & References

This page documents every equation the track standing-start simulator uses, which input variable feeds each one, and where it comes from. Equations taken from the literature cite their source. Where the tool makes its own modelling choice, the equation is tagged Model assumption so you can see exactly what is established physics and what is a simplification.

2. Equation of motion & numerical integration

The bike's speed along the black line, v, and distance, x, follow Newton's second law written as a force balance on the wheel path. This is the same structure as the validated road-cycling power model of Martin et al. [1], applied to sprint efforts by forward integration in [2].

meff dvdt = Fprop − Faero − Frr − Fbear,    dxdt = v
(1)

Martin et al. 1998 [1]Martin et al. 2006 [2] Each force is defined in equations 14–21. If the net force is negative at v = 0, acceleration is held at 0 (the rider can't roll backwards out of the gate).

The ODEs are integrated with Heun's method (explicit trapezoidal / RK2):

vn+1 = vn + Δt2[a(tn, xn, vn) + a(tn+1, x̃, ṽ)],   ṽ = vn + Δt·an,   x̃ = xn + Δt·vn
(2)

Numerical analysis [18] Δt = 0.01 s for the first 30 s, then 0.05 s (distance events) or 0.1 s (Hour). The finish time is found by linear interpolation of the step that crosses X. Halving or quintupling Δt changes results by < 0.001 s.

3. Mass & wheel inertia

Accelerating the wheels' rotation costs energy too. Expressing that as an equivalent translational mass:

meff = mrider + mbike + Irw2,    rw = Ct2π
(3)

Martin et al. 1998 [1] The kinetic-energy term in [1] is ½(m + I/r²)v²; equation 3 is its derivative form. I is the combined moment of inertia of both wheels.

4. Gearing, rollout & cadence

On a fixed gear the cranks are locked to the wheel, so cadence is a direct function of speed:

D = NcNs · Ct    (rollout, m per crank revolution)
(4)
c = 60 vD  (rpm),    ω = 2πc60  (rad/s),    gear inches = NcNs · Ctπ · 25.4
(5)
r = ccp = 60 vD cp,    vpk = cp D60
(6)

Kinematics r is cadence relative to your peak-power cadence. vpk is the speed at which you reach peak power, which is how the tool reports when and where in the race the peak happens: the first time r ≥ 1.

5. Torque–cadence (force–velocity) envelope

Maximal crank torque falls approximately linearly with pedalling rate, so maximal power is a parabola in cadence. This is well established for sprint cycling [4], [5], [6]:

Tmax(c) = T0 (1 − cc0),    c0 = 2cp
(7)
Pmax(c) = Tmax ω = Ppk · r (2 − r)
(8)
T0 = 2 Ppkωp,    ωp = 2πcp60
(9)

Sargeant et al. 1981 [4]Martin et al. 1997 [5]Dorel et al. 2005 [6] Power is zero at 0 rpm, peaks at cp (where r = 1), and falls beyond it. This is why the standing start is torque-limited: at low cadence you can't produce high power however hard you push.

The "constant-torque start" curve replaces the rising half of eq. 8:

Pmax(c) = Ppk · r  for r ≤ 1;    Ppk · r(2 − r)  for r > 1;    T0 = Ppk / ωp
(10)

Model assumption A softer, lower-torque alternative; not a published relationship.

6. Standing → seated transition

A blend fraction s moves from 0 (standing) to 1 (seated) over the transition time after you sit down. If the out-of-saddle duration is given in metres or pedal revs, tsit is the moment x reaches that distance (pedal revs × D).

s(t) = 0  for t < tsit;    min(1, t − tsitttr)  otherwise
(11)
CDA(t) = CDAst + s(t) · (CDAse − CDAst)
(12)
φ(t) = 1 − s(t) · (1 − φse),    Penv = φ(t) · Pmax(c)
(13)

Model assumption A linear blend is a simplification of the real sit-down. The seated capacity factor φse reflects the well-known observation that standing allows higher sprint power at low cadence, but the single constant is this tool's choice. Set it to 100 % to remove the effect.

7. Propulsive force & drivetrain efficiency

The rider's planned crank power Pplan(t) (§12) is converted to a force at the wheel, and can never exceed the envelope:

Fprop = min( η Pplanv,  η Penvv ),    Penvv = φ Ppk 60D cp(2 − r)
(14)

Martin et al. 1998 [1] Writing the envelope as Penv/v keeps the force finite at v = 0: it equals the maximal starting torque T0 transmitted through the gear. The start therefore needs no artificial force cap. For the custom-data curve only the zero-speed limit η·2Ppk·60/(Dcp) is applied.

Pcrank = Fprop vη,    Ploss = (1 − η) Pcrank
(15)

Martin et al. 1998 [1]Spicer et al. 2001 [16] Chain-drive efficiency measured at about 98 % and higher under high loads [16]. All reported powers are crank powers, the same as a crank-based power meter.

8. Aerodynamic drag

Drag power uses the speed of the body through the air. On straights that's the wheel speed. In bends the rider leans, so the body travels a tighter, slower line than the wheels (eq. 18).

Paero = ½ ρ CDA(t) (kv)3,    Faero = Paerov = ½ ρ CDA k3 v2
(16)

Martin et al. 1998 [1]Fitton & Symons 2018 [11] Indoor air is assumed still (no wind term). k = 1 when bend effects are off or on the straights.

tan θ = v2 κg   ⇒   sin θ = q√(1 + q2),   q = v2 κg
(17)
k = RCoMR = 1 − h κ sin θ
(18)

Fitton & Symons 2018 [11] Lean angle θ balances gravity against the centripetal acceleration v²κ (curvature κ = 1/radius). The centre of mass at height h sits h·sinθ inside the wheel line. Model assumption Using a single CoM height for the drag speed (rather than integrating over the body) is a simplification.

9. Rolling resistance & bearing friction

Frr = Crr · m g · n
(19)
n = Nmg = √(1 + q2) = √(1 + (v2κ / g)2)
(20)

Martin et al. 1998 [1]Fitton & Symons 2018 [11] Rolling resistance is proportional to tyre load. In a bend the load is the resultant of gravity and the centripetal force, so n > 1 (about 1.45 at 55 km/h on a 23 m bend). On straights n = 1. Wheel mass is not counted separately because m includes the bike.

Fbear = (91 + 8.7 v) × 10−3  N
(21)

Dahn et al. 1991 [3], as used in Martin et al. 1998 [1] Wheel-bearing friction; only a few watts at race speed.

10. Air density

From temperature, station pressure and humidity, treating air as an ideal mixture of dry air and water vapour:

ρ = p − pvRd TK + pvRv TK,   pv = RH100 es,   Rd = 287.058,  Rv = 461.495 J/(kg·K),  TK = TC + 273.15
(22)

Ideal-gas mixture; cf. Picard et al. 2008 (CIPM-2007) [14] The rigorous CIPM formula adds compressibility and enhancement factors. These change ρ by well under 0.1 % at velodrome conditions.

The saturation vapour pressure es (hPa) uses the Buck equation:

es = 6.1121 · exp[ (18.678 − TC234.5) · TC257.14 + TC ]
(22b)

Buck 1981 [13] These are the revised coefficients Buck later published for water above 0 °C.

The optional elevation helper estimates station pressure with the standard atmosphere:

p = 1013.25 · (1 − 2.25577 × 10−5 z)5.25588  hPa
(23)

ICAO / ISO 2533 Standard Atmosphere [15] This ignores the day's weather; enter the real barometer reading when you have it.

11. Velodrome geometry

A lap is modelled as straight → bend → straight → bend. Each bend is a constant-radius arc on the black line with a transition spiral at each end, where curvature rises smoothly from 0 to 1/R. Each bend turns the bike through π radians:

Larc = πR − Lt,    B = πR + Lt  (bend length),    S = L2 − B  (straight length)
(24)
κ(u) = 12R(1 − cos πuLt)  in a transition;    1R  on the arc;    0  on straights
(25)

Model assumption Real tracks use clothoid-type transitions whose exact shape varies by builder. The cosine ramp is a smooth stand-in that turns through exactly π per bend (checked numerically). Start positions: the finish line is placed 6 m before the end of the home straight (UCI tracks put it near the end of a straight), and the pursuit line is at mid-straight, s0 = S/2.

Riding above the black line. Race distance x is measured on the black (measurement) line, 20 cm above the inside edge. If your wheels run on a parallel line d metres higher, that line's curvature and your progress along the black line are:

κd = κ1 + d κ,    dxdt = v1 + d κ(x)
(25b)
Δslap = ∮ d κ dx = d · (total turning angle) = 2πd
(25c)

Parallel-curve geometry Eq. 25b replaces dx/dt = v in eq. 1, and κd replaces κ in eqs. 17–20. Speed v, drag, rolling resistance and kinetic energy all refer to the line you actually ride, so the energy balance (eq. 37) still closes. Eq. 25c holds for any bend shape, because the two bends together turn through 2π. Riding 10 cm high costs 0.63 m per lap, and the red sprinters' line (85 cm from the inside edge, 65 cm above the black line) costs 4.1 m per lap. The Black-line cost panel re-runs the event at several heights with the same pacing rule. Almost all of the time lost comes from the extra distance. The gentler bend (less lean, lighter tyre load) changes it by well under 1 %: about 0.64 s for 50 cm in the default kilo, with or without bend physics. Model assumption d is an average height held through the bends. The straights are taken as parallel to the black line, so weaving up and down on them is not counted.

12. Pacing curves & the level solver

Every curve except the custom file starts with an all-out phase where Pplan = ∞, so the rider is limited only by the envelope (eq. 14). For the cadence-triggered curves this phase ends at the first moment r ≥ 1 (or at 60 s), giving time ta, power Pa and progress πa. Progress is π = x/X for distance events and t/Tdur for the Hour. L is the single free level each curve solves for.

L(π) = L · [1 + δ(π − ½)]    (plateau with drift; δ = 0 except the settle curve)
(26)
Exponential fade:   Pplan = L(π) + [Pa − L(πa)] · e−(t − ta)/τ
(27)
Linear fade:   Pplan = Pa + (L − Pa) · π − πa1 − πa
(28)
Settle:   Pplan = L(π) + [Pa − L(πa)] · ½[1 + cos(π · min(1, (t − ta)/ts))]
(29)
Time ramp (0 ≤ t ≤ tr):   Pplan = Ppk [p0 + (1 − p0) t/tr],   then eq. 27 with ta = tr
(30)
Even power:   Pplan = L from t = 0 (still capped by eq. 14)
(31)
Custom file:   Pplan(t) = L · Pdata(t)  (linear interpolation between 1-s samples; L = 1 unless scaling)
(32)

Model assumption These are phenomenological pacing shapes, not physiological laws. All of them are bounded by the published torque–cadence envelope (§5), so the physics of the start is the same whichever shape you pick.

The level L is solved so the effort's mean crank power equals your target:

P = 1Tfinish ∫0Tfinish Pcrank(t) dt    solved for L by Illinois false position
(33)

Dowell & Jarratt 1971 [17] Tolerance 0.02 W. This is the time-weighted average a power meter reports, start included.

13. Physiology: critical power, W′ and W′ balance

Critical power (CP) and the finite work capacity above it (W′) come from the classic two-parameter model:

P = CP + W′tlim
(34)

Monod & Scherrer 1965 [9] The simulator uses CP and W′ through equations 35 and 36. You can enter them directly or fit them to your best efforts (below).

Fitting CP and W′ to best efforts. Multiplying eq. 34 by t gives a straight line in work against time. With n = 2–4 best efforts (ti, Pi), the tool fits that line by ordinary least squares:

Wi = Pi ti = W′ + CP · ti,    CP = Σ(ti − t̄)(Wi − W̄)Σ(ti − t̄)2,    W′ = W̄ − CP · t̄
(34b)
s = √[ Σei2 / (n − 2) ],    SE(CP) = s√Σ(ti − t̄)2,    SE(W′) = s √[ 1n + t̄2Σ(ti − t̄)2 ]
(34c)

Hill 1993 [19]Jones et al. 2010 [20] ei are the residuals in work. Standard errors need at least three efforts, and the tool shows them as a percentage of CP and W′. The two-parameter model fits efforts of roughly 2–15 min best. Very short efforts are limited by peak power and very long ones by fatigue the model ignores, so the tool flags efforts outside that range. Model assumption A fit is flagged as loose when SE(CP) > 5 % or SE(W′) > 20 %.

The tool also estimates the best even power for the selected event. It solves Morton's three-parameter model (consistent with eq. 36) for the constant power that exhausts you at the event's duration T, using peak power off the line as Pmax:

T = W′P − CP − W′Ppk − CP   ⇒   P(T) = CP + W′T + W′ / (Ppk − CP)
(34d)

Morton 1996 [7] This is the best constant power you could hold for T, so it makes a sensible target average. Under the same model, a front-loaded all-out effort can average more, because available power stays above P early on and W′ is never fully spent. The torque–cadence limit at the start works the other way. The CP / W′ optimiser (eq. 42) resolves both. The fitted CP and W′ feed eqs. 35, 36 and 42 exactly like entered values. Beyond about 20–30 min, sustainable power falls below CP [20], so eq. 34d overestimates the Hour.

W′ balance is tracked with the differential model of Skiba et al.:

dW′baldt = −(P − CP)  if P > CP;    (CP − P) · W′0 − W′balW′0  otherwise
(35)

Skiba et al. 2015 [8] If W′bal goes below zero on a non-W′ curve, the tool warns that the plan probably isn't sustainable.

For the W′ curves and the CP/W′ optimiser, available power also declines as W′ is spent, following Morton's three-parameter model. It is combined with the torque–cadence shape:

Penv = φ · r(2 − r) · [ CP + (Ppk − CP) W′balW′0 ]
(36)

Morton 1996 [7] In [7] maximal power falls linearly from Pmax toward CP as W′ is used. Model assumption Multiplying that ceiling by the cadence shape r(2 − r) combines [7] with [4]–[6]. For the "W′-paced" curve the cap applies during the all-out start only, and L is solved so that min W′bal = 0 exactly at the finish.

14. Energy budget & reported outputs

η ∫Pcrank dt = ∫Faerov dt + ∫Frrv dt + ∫Fbearv dt + ½ meff vend2
(37)

Work–energy theoremMartin et al. 1998 [1] The tool shows this balance and the residual (typically 0.0 J) as a check on the integrator. Kinetic energy at the finish is energy you carried over the line.

Peak-power time:   tpk = min{ t : r(t) ≥ 1 },   distance x(tpk),   pedal revolutions x(tpk)/D
(38)
Split power:   Pi = E(ti) − E(ti−1)ti − ti−1,   E(t) = ∫0t Pcrank dt
(39)
WCDA = PCDAse,    sensitivity:  ΔT = T(θ + Δθ) − T(θ)  with L re-solved
(40)

Tool outputs The "What is it worth?" table perturbs one input θ at a time and re-runs the full simulation, so the deltas include every interaction (for example, a lower CdA also changes when you reach peak cadence).

15. Optimal power distribution

The optimiser searches a family of plans: all-out (envelope-limited) until ta, then the exponential settle of eq. 27 with δ = 0, optionally with the sit-down time tsit as a free variable too.

Average-power constraint:   minta, τ [, tsit] Tfinish   s.t. eq. 33 holds,   Pplan ≥ Pfloor = f · P
(41)
CP / W′ constraint:   minta, τ, L [, tsit] Tfinish   s.t. P ≤ Penv of eq. 36 throughout
(42)

Nelder & Mead 1965 [10]de Koning et al. 1999 [12] Bounds are enforced with a logistic transform, and two starting points are tried. For the Hour the objective is to maximise distance instead. The result matches the theory in [12]: accelerate as hard as possible, then ride evenly, with all-out being optimal in short events. Model assumption The floor f (default 50 %) is a practical guard against an unrideable "sprint then coast" answer. Holding the plateau even (δ = 0) deliberately ignores the small gain from bleeding off speed before the line.

What the model leaves out: cadence-dependent efficiency at sub-maximal power, tyre scrub and steering losses, changes in body kinetic energy as you lean in and out of bends, track-surface temperature effects on Crr, drafting, and reaction time at the gate. If your CdA or Crr came from on-track testing using wheel speed, the bend effects (eqs. 17–20) are already baked in. Switch them off to avoid counting them twice.
